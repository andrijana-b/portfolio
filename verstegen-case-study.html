01 · Outcome

+€10K monthly webshop revenue after moving to one Shopify store

Giving consumers (B2C) and professional chefs (B2B) one Shopify store, organised around what they cook, grew Verstegen's monthly webshop revenue by more than €10K.

That growth came from three changes customers could feel: they found products faster, product pages answered their questions, and professional (B2B) customers, who used to be redirected to a separate webshop, now buy in the same store as consumers.

02 · Context and problem

Hard-to-find products, a buggy checkout and a separate shop for B2C and B2B

Verstegen is a Dutch company that has made herbs, spices and sauces since 1886. Most people know it from the supermarket shelf, but it also supplies professional kitchens and the food industry. Its webshop had to bring that range online for both consumers and professionals

Customers who already wanted to buy couldn’t find the product, lost their order at a broken checkout, or got sent to another website, and Verstegen needed its agency for every fix.

03 · Key decisions

Three key decisions: what I chose, what I rejected and why

  1. 01

    I chose

    A category structure built from user interviews and card sorting

    I rejected

    Moving the existing catalogue structure to Shopify

    Because

    The old structure mirrored how Verstegen thinks internally. Customers think about what they're cooking.

  2. 02

    I chose

    One store for consumers (B2C) and professionals (B2B)

    I rejected

    Keeping the separate professional site

    Because

    Customers named the split as a problem, and two shops meant two brands and two scattered data sets.

  3. 03

    I chose

    A short, persuasive top of the product page with details in expandable sections

    I rejected

    One long page with everything on it

    Because

    Growth had to come from mobile, where a long page pushes the buy button and the proof out of sight.

1. Navigation and category structure built from user interviews and card sorting

The easy route was to copy the old catalogue into Shopify. But that structure was organised by product line and packaging, which is how Verstegen sees its range, not how people shop for it. Missing categorisation was also the client's biggest complaint, and only customers could tell us how they expect herbs, spices and sauces to be grouped.

So I started with interviews to learn what people look for, what they call things and where they'd look. Card sorting then showed which groupings felt natural. The live menu reflects that:

  • Single spices, with the ten most common named directly in the menu
  • Blends by what you cook: chicken, meat, fish, vegetables and BBQ
  • Pepper and salt as their own group
  • Sauces by type
  • Dietary entry points: low sodium, no added salt and organic

I chose a mega menu so customers see all these groups in one view, and so Verstegen can keep adding products and categories as the range grows, without redesigning the navigation.

2. One Shopify store for B2C and B2B, with one basket and one checkout

Professionals used to be redirected to a separate site. Customers had flagged that split themselves. For Verstegen it meant two brand experiences, two shops to maintain and customer data in two places, while better e-commerce insight was one of its main goals.

I removed the redirect and brought both audiences into one Shopify store with one navigation, one basket and one checkout. The result is one brand, one platform and one data set that shows how both groups actually shop.

3. Product pages designed for a confident buying decision on mobile

Before designing any page, the CRO specialist and I agreed on one goal per page type. That let us judge design choices against a goal instead of taste. For the product page, the goal was a confident buying decision.

The client said product pages were too thin, so the obvious fix was to add everything to one long page. I rejected that. On a phone, every extra block pushes the buy button and the proof further down. Instead, the top of the page carries what persuades, and the details sit in expandable sections customers open when they need them.

  • USPs near the price, such as order before 23:59 for next-day delivery, repeated across the page so they stay in view
  • Trust signals: quality since 1886, the Kiyoh rating, Thuiswinkel and B Corp, to lower the perceived risk of buying
  • Reviews close to the decision, not buried at the bottom
  • A bundle section with products that go well together, to raise average order value and make it easy to buy everything for one dish at once
  • Prices without a euro sign, a small CRO choice to soften the "pain of paying"
  • A second Add to cart button at the end of the desktop page, so people who read everything don't have to scroll back up to buy

04 · Why Shopify

Why Verstegen moved from WooCommerce to Shopify

Verstegen moved from WooCommerce to Shopify to get a webshop that breaks less often, that staff without technical knowledge can manage themselves, and that can grow with the business without an agency for every change. The client also asked for a design that matches the new brand identity and works much better on mobile.

Moving to Shopify's native checkout also ended the WooCommerce checkout bugs: discount codes that didn't apply, shipping costs that didn't load, an endless spinner after “Place order” and “session expired” errors. Shopify now maintains the checkout, and the Verstegen team, including staff without technical knowledge, can manage the shop themselves. That ended the technical dependence on an agency the client named as one of its biggest challenges.

Client goals: revenue growth, e-commerce insight and findability

  • Revenue growth through the webshop
  • Better e-commerce insight, with better data collection on how people shop
  • Better findability of Verstegen and its products in search engines
  • A future-proof platform with ongoing technical development, ready for new markets

05 · What didn't work

What didn't work: a detailed Figma file without check-ins during the build

The design phase went really well. Goals and user flows were clear from the start, collaboration was strong, and the team called the Figma file detailed and easy to follow. That was exactly the trap.

What I tried. I put my effort into a complete handoff: every page type specced, every asset ready, every state documented. I assumed that file would carry the design through the build.

Why it failed. As the project went on, different developers joined. Some of the original developers moved on without a proper handover, and we had no regular check-ins with the development team. Small differences between design and build only surfaced near launch. The final site didn't fully match the designs, and fixing those details afterwards cost extra time.

What it taught me. A detailed file is not the same as alignment. Everything looked finished on our side, so nobody checked how it was being translated on theirs. Handoff is not a single moment. It's a conversation that has to continue through the build, especially when the people on the other side change.

06 · What I'd do differently

What I'd do differently: a dev review before the build and weekly check-ins

Next time I'd treat the build as part of the design work, not something that happens after it. I'd run a short design review with the developers before development starts, and add a "ready for dev" checklist so nothing leaves Figma without a shared understanding. During the build, I'd hold a short weekly check-in and review each page type on staging as it's built, so small differences get caught while they're cheap to fix. And when developers change, I'd make sure the new people get a walkthrough from me, not just the file.
