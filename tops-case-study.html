01 · Context

One website, many audiences

TOPS is a specialist recruitment and staffing company. It connects people with work across four sectors: Green, Construction, Infrastructure and Agriculture.

The company was refreshing its brand and expanding its services. That meant the website had to do far more than list job vacancies. It had to serve a broad ecosystem of people, each arriving with a different question:

Who What they want to know
Job seekers, temporary workers and freelancers Is there work for me, and how do I get it?
People looking for a permanent position Is this a job I can build on?
Career changers and students Can I get into this sector, and how do I learn the skills?
Professionals looking to develop What's my next step?
Employers looking for staff Can TOPS find me the right people, and do they know my sector?
Existing clients and partners How do I keep working with TOPS?
People who want to work at TOPS itself What is it like to work here?

One website. Many audiences. Four sectors. All of it still had to feel unmistakably like TOPS.

02 · The design challenge

A simple brief with layers underneath

The existing website had cluttered navigation, competing CTAs and weak visual hierarchy, making it difficult for candidates to quickly find and apply for vacancies. Job listings were easy to overlook, while limited social proof reduced trust.

The platform was also rigid, making it difficult for the internal team to create, manage and scale new pages independently. The new website therefore needed to improve the candidate experience while providing a more flexible, scalable foundation for the team.

At first glance, the brief sounded straightforward:

The overall website should feel like TOPS. But as users move deeper into a sector, the interface should take on that sector's visual identity.

In reality, there were several layers of complexity underneath.

01

One website, four visual identities

TOPS has four sector labels: Groen, Agri, Infra and Bouw. Each has its own colour and character. Together they still had to read as one brand.

02

Many audiences, many journeys

A student exploring a training programme and an employer looking for a construction crew need very different things. Both land on the same website.

03

Different stages of the funnel

Some visitors are just discovering TOPS. Others are one click away from applying. The design had to work for both, from discovery to consideration to conversion.

04

Built to be managed, not just admired

The website would be built in Storyblok, a content management system. The TOPS team needed to create and change pages themselves, without a designer redrawing every new page.

The challenge wasn't to design a collection of attractive pages. It was this:

How do we create a strong, recognisable brand experience, while giving TOPS the flexibility to keep building new pages and content over time?

03 · My approach

Structure, system, expression

After many years of working on websites like this, I've learned that problems start when brand, content and usability are designed all at once and blur together. So I split the work into three levels, and worked through them in order.

01

Structure

What does the user need to accomplish? Finding a job, understanding a training route, contacting TOPS as an employer.

02

System

Which components and patterns support that task? The reusable blocks the pages are made from: cards, search, forms, calls to action.

03

Expression

How does the TOPS or sector identity show up within that system? Colour, accents, imagery and tone.

This order mattered. It kept the visual identity from becoming more important than usability. The brand gets to shine, but never at the cost of someone finding a job or getting in touch.

04 · From pages to a system

Designing the building blocks, not the pages

The brief listed 14 page types: homepage, vacancy listing, vacancy detail, application, job seeker landing page, sector pages, training pages, employer pages, sector-specific employer pages, About TOPS, Working at TOPS, contact, blog overview and blog detail.

The obvious route was to design those 14 pages one by one. But I reframed the goal:

The goal was not "Design 14 pages." It was "Design the system that allows TOPS to create many more pages."

Instead of treating them as isolated designs, I looked at the patterns they shared and turned those into reusable building blocks. Think of it like LEGO: a fixed set of well-made pieces that can be combined into many different builds, and every build still looks like it belongs.

The set included hero sections, intro and content blocks, image + text sections, cards and card grids, vacancy components, search and filtering, CTAs, testimonials, accordions, forms, blog components and application elements.

Designing for Storyblok from the start

Storyblok is where the TOPS content team builds pages, by stacking predefined blocks. So every block I designed in Figma had to make sense as a block an editor would pick and fill in.

That meant each block had to work not only on the page where I first designed it, but in any context. I tested every block against:

  • long and short headlines
  • more or less text than expected
  • different image ratios
  • different combinations of buttons
  • desktop, tablet and mobile
  • all four sector colours
  • reuse across different page types

The payoff came on two levels. For the design team, new layouts no longer started from zero. For the content team, they could build new pages themselves and stay within the visual system, without needing a designer each time.

The design system became the bridge between design and content management.

05 · One system, four expressions

Groen, Agri, Infra and Bouw from one set of parts

A big part of the work was taking one component and making it work for Groen, Agri, Infra and Bouw.

The tempting option was four separate designs. That would have made the website feel like four unrelated sites, and multiplied the work for designers and developers by four.

Instead: one system, four expressions.

Stays the same in every sector Changes per sector
Structure and layout Colour
Spacing Accent and selected states
Typography and hierarchy Labels and highlights
How things work (interaction) Buttons
Component behaviour Supporting imagery

The things on the right are controlled through design tokens: named settings, like "accent colour", that each sector fills in with its own value. Change the token, and every button, label and highlight in that sector follows.

The identity follows the user through the site. Take this path:

  1. TOPS
  2. Groen
  3. Werkzoeken
  4. Opleidingen

The top level feels like TOPS. Once someone enters Groen, the Groen identity carries through every page beneath it.

The sector colours weren't decoration. They became part of the navigation: a quiet, constant signal of which part of TOPS you're in.

06 · Designing for the recruitment funnel

More focus the closer people get to applying

The website isn't a static set of information pages. People move through it in stages, and at each stage they're asking a different question.

The further down the funnel, the more focused the interface becomes. Early pages help people orient and get inspired. Later pages remove distractions, so one action stands out. This shaped the CTA hierarchy, how much information each page shows, navigation, content priority, form design and supporting information.

Three journeys, one system

Within that funnel, each audience follows its own route:

Job seekers

  1. Discover
  2. Explore
  3. Find a vacancy
  4. Understand the role
  5. Apply

Career changers and students

  1. Discover
  2. Understand the opportunity
  3. Explore the programme
  4. Understand the career path
  5. Take action

In the Green sector this covers training programmes and work-study routes, so it's a longer journey that needs more explanation and reassurance.

Employers

  1. Understand TOPS
  2. Explore the service
  3. Understand the sector expertise
  4. Get in touch

All three are built from the same blocks. No separate experiences, just different combinations of the same parts.

07 · The vacancy experience

Where a visitor becomes an applicant

The vacancy page is where a visitor becomes an applicant. That makes it one of the most important pages on the site.

Vacancy pages are often treated as long blocks of text. But a job seeker usually needs answers to a handful of questions, fast:

  • What is the job?
  • Where is it?
  • How many hours?
  • What will I earn?
  • What experience do I need?
  • What will I actually do?
  • How do I apply?

So I designed the page around those questions. The key facts sit at the top, easy to scan, so people can decide within seconds whether a job is for them. The fuller description follows for those who want to read on.

The apply button stays visible and prominent the whole way down. Whenever someone is ready, the next step is right there.

08 · Translating the new brand into digital

From brand guidelines to a working interface

The new brand guidelines set the visual direction. But guidelines describe how a brand looks on a poster or a business card. A website also has to behave: it scrolls, it responds to taps, it reshapes itself for a phone.

I used the guidelines to define how the identity works on screen, across typography, colour, buttons, cards, spacing, photography, content hierarchy, calls to action, sector styling and responsive layouts.

The question for every choice was the same: does this make the brand recognisable and make the site easier to use? Where those two pulled apart, usability won.

09 · Outcome

A scalable UX/UI foundation

The result was a scalable UX/UI foundation for the new TOPS website. The project delivered:

  • One consistent TOPS experience across desktop and mobile
  • Four sector identities, Groen, Agri, Infra and Bouw, built on one shared system
  • A library of reusable blocks and patterns, ready to use in Storyblok
  • Designs for every key journey: job seekers, career changers, employers and content readers
  • Independence for the TOPS team: new pages can be built from existing blocks, without redesigning the experience from scratch

10 · What I learned

Consistency doesn't mean identical

The most important lesson: consistency doesn't mean making everything look identical.

The real work was finding the balance between three forces:

Consistency

Users should recognise the same product everywhere and understand how it works.

Differentiation

Each sector should have its own identity and feel relevant to its audience.

Scalability

The design should work across many pages without adding complexity for designers and developers.

Push too hard on any one of them and the others break. The answer was not four separate websites. It was one set of rules, flexible enough to express four sectors.

Final reflection

This project pushed me to think beyond individual screens, and to see how everything connects:

  1. Brand
  2. Information architecture
  3. Components
  4. Sector identities
  5. User journeys
  6. Conversion

The most valuable thing I designed wasn't any single page. It was the rules behind the pages: enough structure to keep the experience consistent, and enough freedom for TOPS to grow.

One brand. Four sector identities. Reusable blocks. A system that can evolve.
